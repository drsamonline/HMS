# Medical officer

You will spend most of your time in one screen, moving between patients who are
waiting and patients whose notes you are writing. The software is designed to keep
that moving.

## What you see when you log in

**Your Clinic.** Today's queue, your patients, and the inpatient list if your hospital
has inpatients.

## Your first hour

1. Open **OPD Queue**. It shows who is waiting and how long.
2. Open one patient and read their record: the case record, previous visits, the last
   prescription.
3. Write a test case record on a test patient. Note that the record saves as a draft
   until you complete it - nothing is lost if you are called away.
4. Create a prescription with one medicine. Note that your name and NCH registration
   number appear on it. If the number is missing, the prescription says so, and you
   should tell the administrator rather than print it.

## Your first week

- Learn the follow-up form. When you change a treatment, it asks **why**. That is not
  a formality: the NCH Code of Ethics requires the reason to be stated at the next
  consultation, and the software will not save a change without it.
- Learn to print the whole case before a patient leaves. It is one button and it is
  the document they take away.
- Learn what the queue does when you are running late. It does not hide you; it shows
  the wait honestly, which is better than a patient being told "any minute now".

## What you must never do

- **Never record a treatment change without its reason.** The software refuses it, and
  working around the refusal by recording the reason as "as discussed" defeats the
  point.
- **Never prescribe under a colleague's login.** The registration number on the
  prescription is a professional statement and it must be yours.
- **Never leave a case record as a draft at the end of the day.** A draft is not part
  of the patient's record.

## What the software will not let you do

You cannot see bills, you cannot change a patient's demographics, and you cannot
delete a record you created. Corrections are additions, not erasures - which is what
makes the record defensible.
